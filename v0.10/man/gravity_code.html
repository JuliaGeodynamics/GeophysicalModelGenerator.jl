Gravity code ​

The voxel_grav function computes gravity anomalies and their horizontal gradients from a 3D density model, on a flat Earth (arrays or CartData) or on the reference ellipsoid (GeoData), at the grid columns or at arbitrary stations. Every grid cell acts as a point mass at its centre, and cells close to a station are subdivided. The anomaly is linear in the density, and voxel_grav_adjoint computes the adjoint, i.e. the sensitivity of the anomaly to the density. Together they can be used to compute free-air and Bouguer anomalies, sensitivity maps, and deterministic or probabilistic (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) inversions with general-purpose Julia packages. normal_gravity, bouguer_correction and topographic_effect turn observed gravity into anomalies that can be compared with the models.

This page explains the forward computation and then works through five examples: free-air and Bouguer anomalies of a volcano, a sensitivity map, a least-squares inversion, a probabilistic inversion with Turing.jl, and a probabilistic inversion with one parameter per grid point.

Forward computation ​

julia
using GeophysicalModelGenerator
X, Y, Z = xyz_grid(range(-20, 20, 41), range(-20, 20, 41), range(-15, 0, 16))   # km
RHO = fill(2700.0, size(X))
RHO[(abs.(X) .< 5) .& (abs.(Y) .< 5) .& (-9 .< Z .< -3)] .= 3000.0
dg, gradX, gradY = voxel_grav(X, Y, Z, RHO; refMod = "NE", lengthUnit = "km", outName = "Bouguer")

  • The grid must be rectilinear: X varies only along the first dimension, Y along the second, Z along the third. The spacing may vary along each direction, e.g. finer cells near the surface. CartData and GeoData grids may also be curvilinear, with levels that follow the topography (see Curvilinear grids).

  • dg [mGal] is computed at the x, y coordinates of the grid, at the height zRef. This is either a number (default 0) or an array with a height for every surface point, e.g. the topography. Only the part of the model below maximum(zRef) is used. Arbitrary stations are possible with GeoData and CartData grids (see below).

  • gradX, gradY are the horizontal gradients ∂dg/∂x, ∂dg/∂y [mGal/m; 1 mGal/m = 10⁴ Eötvös], computed analytically. gradients = false skips them, which saves time.

  • refMod is the reference density for every depth. It is subtracted from RHO before the anomaly is computed.

  • cutoff ignores cells whose horizontal distance from a station is larger than cutoff (in the unit of the coordinates). Codes that model density structures, such as Harmonica and SimPEG, sum all cells, which is the default here as well; a cutoff is standard for terrain corrections, which conventionally extend to 166.7 km (Hayford–Bowie zone O). Cells beyond the cutoff are not visited on the CPU, which saves time over large areas.

  • subdivide controls the accuracy close to the stations (see below); subdivide = 0 switches it off.

  • outName = nothing writes no Paraview file, which is what you want inside an inversion loop.

  • RHO may have any number type, so that automatic differentiation (ForwardDiff) works through voxel_grav.

Accuracy. Every cell is represented by a point mass at its centre plus its quadrupole term, i.e. the second moments of the cell (its edge lengths squared over 12). The error of this approximation decreases with the fourth power of the ratio of cell size to distance; for a single flat cell it is 0.06%, 0.008% and 0.0001% at a distance of 1.5, 2.5 and 8 times its size (a point mass alone: 3%, 1.1% and 0.1%). Cells closer to a station than subdivide = 2.5 times their size are split recursively into halves (along their longer directions) until every part is far enough, as in the tesseroid code of Uieda et al. (2016). This is a separate correction for the few cells near the stations. As it depends only on the geometry, it is computed once and stored (for the last 4 geometries), so repeated calls with the same grid and stations, as in inversions, cost the same as without subdivision. For a 1 km thick slab directly below a station with 4 km wide cells, the error is 0.03%. Compared with Harmonica, the deviation is 2e-6 to 1e-5 of the maximum anomaly for prisms and 7e-6 to 3e-5 for tesseroids (see Benchmark).

Cost. The cost is proportional to (number of stations) × (number of anomalous cells). Cells with abs(RHO - refMod) < rhoTol are skipped, so models in which only a part of the domain differs from the reference model are fast. By default the main loop runs on a single CPU core (the subdivision on all threads); see Parallel and GPU computation.

Reference. The array version and CartData grids assume a flat Earth, with Z and zRef relative to the datum of your model, usually sea level (the GMT topography used by import_topo is relative to mean sea level). This is fine for models up to a few hundred kilometres wide; for larger areas use a GeoData grid, which is computed on the reference ellipsoid (see Geographic grids on the ellipsoid). In both cases the result is the attraction of the density difference RHO - refMod, i.e. the gravity disturbance caused by it. Observed anomalies are computed with respect to normal gravity on a reference ellipsoid (e.g. GRS80), so they can be compared with modelled ones, with two caveats. First, a gravity disturbance is defined at the station height above the ellipsoid, whereas a classical gravity anomaly uses the height above the geoid; the difference (about 0.3 mGal per metre of geoid height) is nearly constant over a regional model. Second, a long-wavelength difference between data and model is usually removed as a regional trend, because refMod is not the density structure of the reference Earth.

Parallel and GPU computation ​

voxel_grav and voxel_grav_adjoint can run their kernels with KernelAbstractions.jl, an optional dependency, on all CPU threads or on a GPU. Pass a backend, and for GPUs without double precision (Apple Metal) float_type = Float32:

julia
using GeophysicalModelGenerator, Metal                    # Metal loads KernelAbstractions
dg, = voxel_grav(X, Y, Z, RHO; refMod = "NE", lengthUnit = "km", outName = nothing, backend = MetalBackend(), float_type = Float32)
sens = voxel_grav_adjoint(X, Y, Z, R; lengthUnit = "km", backend = MetalBackend(), float_type = Float32)

CUDABackend() (CUDA.jl) and ROCBackend() (AMDGPU.jl) should work the same way, also with Float64 (not tested yet); CPU() (using KernelAbstractions) uses all threads of Julia (julia -t auto). The subdivision of cells near the stations runs on the CPU threads, and the results are returned as ordinary Float64 arrays. On the GPU, cutoff is applied as a mask, so it does not save time there.

The Float32 results differ from the Float64 ones by a relative error of about 1e-5 to 1e-4, far below typical data errors. For HMC and iterative inversions, which need hundreds to thousands of forward and adjoint computations, this is where the time goes; see Benchmark for timings.

Geographic grids on the ellipsoid ​

For a GeoData grid (longitude, latitude, depth, as created by lonlatdepth_grid), voxel_grav and voxel_grav_adjoint take the curvature of the Earth into account. Every cell is a point mass at its position on the reference ellipsoid (WGS84 by default), its volume is computed from the radii of curvature of the ellipsoid, and the anomaly is the component of the attraction along the ellipsoid normal at each station; the gradients are taken towards east and north. For a CartData grid (x, y, z in km) the flat-Earth computation is used. Both return (dg, dgdx, dgdy) (the gradients only with gradients = true, otherwise nothing), and take refMod, zRef, cutoff (in km), subdivide, rhoTol, backend and float_type as above:

julia
Topo = import_topo(lon = [14, 16], lat = [36.8, 38.8], file = "@earth_relief_30s")
Lon, Lat, Depth = lonlatdepth_grid(14.5:0.01:15.5, 37.3:0.01:38.2, -20:0.25:3.5)    # depth in km
Grid = GeoData(Lon, Lat, Depth, (z = Depth,))
topo, = interpolate_datafields_2D(Topo, Lon[:, :, 1], Lat[:, :, 1])                # topography [km]

RHO = fill(2670.0, size(Lon))
RHO[Depth .> topo] .= 0.0                                                         # air
ρ_ref = [z > 0 ? 0.0 : 2670.0 for z in Depth[1, 1, :]]
FA, = voxel_grav(Grid, RHO; refMod = ρ_ref, zRef = topo)                          # free-air anomaly [mGal]
sens = voxel_grav_adjoint(Grid, ones(size(FA)); zRef = topo)

Stations. Instead of the grid columns, the anomaly can be computed at arbitrary stations, e.g. at the points of a gravity survey, given as GeoData (height above the ellipsoid in km as depth) or CartData (in km). The result has the shape of the stations:

julia
Stations = GeoData(lon_st, lat_st, height_st, (name = id_st,))       # vectors of the station coordinates
dg, dgdx, dgdy = voxel_grav(Grid, RHO; refMod = ρ_ref, stations = Stations, gradients = true)

Heights (Depth, zRef) are used as heights above the ellipsoid. Heights relative to sea level can be used as they are: the geoid undulation (less than 100 m) shifts stations and masses together and hardly changes the modelled anomaly. ellipsoid = (a = 6371e3, f = 0) computes on a sphere, and other values allow other planets.

Curvilinear grids ​

CartData and GeoData grids may be curvilinear, i.e. their levels follow the topography or internal interfaces, as created by deform_grid and curvilinear_grid (see Curvilinear grids). Such a grid represents the topography without a staircase, and no air cells are needed:

julia
Grid = curvilinear_grid(x, y, (Topo, -5.0, -20.0), (10, 15))      # 10 cells between topography and 5 km depth
dg, = voxel_grav(Grid, RHO; refMod = "AVG")                       # stations on top of the grid

The cells are hexahedra with vertical edges, whose top and bottom are bilinear between the grid points. Their volume, centroid and full tensor of second moments are computed exactly, and the quadrupole term includes the mixed moments, which are not zero when the top or bottom is inclined (with only the diagonal moments, the error at 2.5 cell sizes would be 1.4%, 2.9% and 6.1% of the field of the cell for slopes of 0.25, 0.5 and 1, instead of 0.2%, 0.25% and 0.6%). Cells close to a station are subdivided in their reference coordinates. Compared with an independent reference (the cells integrated as vertical line masses), the error for a grid of three layers that follow a hill with slopes of up to 0.6 is 4e-5 of the maximum anomaly for stations 20 m above the topography and 7e-5 for stations on it.

The stations are on top of the grid columns by default; zRef and stations work as for rectilinear grids. All cells are used, also above a station. The x, y (or longitude, latitude) coordinates must be those of a rectilinear grid, with any spacing (e.g. the nodes of a summation-by-parts grid), and the heights must be monotonic in every column. Densities are given at the grid points, so an interface that follows a grid level is smeared over the cells on both sides of it; give such layers their own cells (as curvilinear_grid does) and the density of the layer at its points.

Gravity corrections ​

Observed gravity is reduced to anomalies with respect to the reference ellipsoid before it is compared with models:

  • normal_gravity(lat, h) [mGal] is the normal gravity of GRS80 at geodetic latitude lat [°] and height h [km] (Somigliana's formula with the second-order height correction). g_obs .- normal_gravity.(lat, h) is the gravity disturbance for ellipsoidal heights, and approximately the free-air anomaly for heights above sea level.

  • bouguer_correction(topo) [mGal] is the simple Bouguer correction 2πGρh (an infinite slab; for the oceans with density_water - density).

  • topographic_effect(Topo) is the complete Bouguer correction, i.e. including the terrain correction: the attraction of the topography (density 2670 kg/m³; oceans as 1040 - 2670 kg/m³) of a GeoData or CartData surface, computed with voxel_grav at stations on the topography or at given stations, up to cutoff = 166.7 km. The topography is a single layer of curvilinear cells between sea level and the surface:

julia
Topo = import_topo(lon = [14, 16], lat = [36.8, 38.8], file = "@earth_relief_30s")
TE, = topographic_effect(Topo; stations = Stations)                   # mGal
CBA = g_obs .- normal_gravity.(lat_st, height_st) .- vec(TE)           # complete Bouguer anomaly

For a 1 km high plateau, 200 km wide, topographic_effect gives 111.44 mGal at its centre (a slab of that width: 111.41 mGal; an infinite slab: 111.97 mGal). For a hill with slopes of up to 0.43 (6561 points), it deviates by 0.008 mGal (4e-5) from the reference, and takes 0.11 s. The earlier version, which discretized the topography with horizontal layers of thickness dz, deviated by 9 to 11 mGal for dz = 100 to 25 m and took 0.24 to 0.87 s.

How much the curvature matters: for a cap of 5° radius (about 550 km), 10 km thick, with a density contrast of 300 kg/m³, the anomaly 20 km above its centre is 124.66 mGal on the sphere (exact: 124.67 mGal), but 120.15 mGal with the flat-Earth approximation, 4% less; at the edge of the cap the difference is 5%. On the other hand, over an area of 0.5° × 0.5°, GeoData and CartData results agree within 0.14%.

Example 1: free-air and Bouguer anomalies of a volcano ​

In many models the topography is available as a surface. Put it on the x, y coordinates of the model grid, e.g. with project_CartData (see the basic tutorial):

julia
Topo = import_topo(lon = [14.5, 15.5], lat = [37.3, 38.2], file = "@earth_relief_03s")    # Etna
proj = ProjectionPoint(Lon = 15.0, Lat = 37.75)
x, y, z = -40:0.5:40, -40:0.5:40, -20:0.125:3.5                                          # km
Topo_rect = project_CartData(CartData(xyz_grid(x, y, 0)), Topo, proj)
topo = Topo_rect.z.val[:, :, 1]                                                          # [km]
X, Y, Z = xyz_grid(x, y, z)

Here we use a synthetic volcano instead, 2.5 km high, with a spherical magma chamber (radius 3 km, 300 kg/m³ lighter than the crust) at 6 km below sea level, because its Bouguer anomaly is known analytically:

julia
X, Y, Z = xyz_grid(-30:0.5:30, -30:0.5:30, -15:0.125:3)                    # km
topo = 2.5 .* exp.(-(X[:, :, 1] .^ 2 .+ Y[:, :, 1] .^ 2) ./ 8^2)            # topography [km]
RHO = fill(2670.0, size(X))
RHO[Z .> topo] .= 0.0                                                        # air
RHO[X .^ 2 .+ Y .^ 2 .+ (Z .+ 6) .^ 2 .< 3^2] .= 2370.0                      # magma chamber

The free-air anomaly is the effect of all masses relative to a reference Earth, here air above sea level and 2670 kg/m³ below, computed at stations on the topography (zRef = topo):

julia
ρ_ref = [z > 0 ? 0.0 : 2670.0 for z in Z[1, 1, :]]
FA, = voxel_grav(X, Y, Z, RHO; refMod = ρ_ref, zRef = topo, Topo = topo, lengthUnit = "km", outName = "FreeAir")

The complete Bouguer anomaly removes the effect of the topography between sea level and the surface, with a reduction density ρ_B, at the same stations. This includes the terrain correction. Since the computation is linear, this is a second call with the topographic masses only:

julia
ρ_B = 2670.0
BC, = voxel_grav(X, Y, Z, ρ_B .* ((Z .> 0) .& (Z .<= topo)); refMod = zeros(size(X, 3)), zRef = topo, lengthUnit = "km", outName = nothing)
CBA = FA .- BC

The simple Bouguer anomaly approximates the topography below every station by an infinite slab and ignores the terrain:

julia
SBA = FA .- 2π * 6.67408e-11 * ρ_B .* (topo .* 1e3) .* 1e5     # mGal; topo in km

The figure shows a section through the model and the three anomalies along it. The free-air anomaly (221 mGal at the summit) is dominated by the topography. The complete Bouguer anomaly (-3.1 mGal at the summit) shows the magma chamber only and agrees with the analytical solution for a sphere within 0.04 mGal. The simple Bouguer anomaly (-59 mGal) is far off, because a slab is a poor approximation of a volcano. topographic_effect computes BC directly from the topography.

The stations lie on the topography, directly on top of the cells, which is where subdivision matters: at the summit the free-air anomaly is 188, 210, 221 and 221 mGal for grid spacings (horizontal × vertical) of 1 km × 500 m, 500 m × 250 m, 500 m × 125 m and 250 m × 125 m, compared with 179, 205, 216 and 218 mGal without subdivision (subdivide = 0). The remaining dependence on the vertical spacing comes from the staircase representation of the topography by the grid, which a curvilinear grid avoids (see Curvilinear grids). The Bouguer anomaly is hardly affected (-3.1 mGal in all cases), because the discretization error of the topography is the same in FA and BC and cancels.

Example 2: sensitivity map ​

voxel_grav_adjoint(X, Y, Z, R) returns, for every grid point, the derivative of sum(R .* dg) with respect to the density there, in mGal per kg/m³. It costs about as much as one forward computation and takes the same lengthUnit and zRef as voxel_grav. With R = 1 at one station and 0 elsewhere, it gives the sensitivity of that station; with R = ones(size(dg)), that of the anomaly summed over all stations:

julia
X, Y, Z = xyz_grid(range(-20, 20, 41), range(-20, 20, 41), range(-15, 0, 16))   # km
e = zeros(41, 41); e[21, 21] = 1                                                # station at x = y = 0
sens_station = voxel_grav_adjoint(X, Y, Z, e; lengthUnit = "km")
write_paraview(CartData(X, Y, Z, (sens_station = sens_station,)), "gravity_sensitivity")

The sensitivity decreases rapidly with distance from the station: a grid point 1 km below the station affects it 42 times more than one 8 km below. Grid points on the boundary of the model have half the sensitivity of their neighbours, because they belong to fewer cells.

A density change δRHO changes the anomaly at the station by sum(sens_station .* δRHO). If the density at every grid point has an independent uncertainty σ_ρ (an array of the same size as X), the resulting uncertainty of the anomaly at that station is sqrt(sum((sens_station .* σ_ρ) .^ 2)).

The sensitivity is that of a model with a fixed reference model (refMod given as a vector). With refMod = "AVG" or a corner ("NE", …), the reference model itself changes with RHO.

Inversion ​

Gravity inversion recovers the density from an observed anomaly dg_obs. Since the forward problem is linear, the forward and adjoint functions are all that general-purpose Julia packages need. Use a fixed reference model, rhoTol = 0 (otherwise the operator is not exactly linear) and outName = nothing:

julia
ref = zeros(size(X, 3))      # RHO is then the density anomaly
forward(ρ) = voxel_grav(X, Y, Z, ρ; refMod = ref, lengthUnit = "km", rhoTol = 0, outName = nothing, printing = false)[1]
adjoint(r) = voxel_grav_adjoint(X, Y, Z, r; lengthUnit = "km")

Gravity data alone cannot determine where the mass sits: a shallow and a deep body can give the same anomaly, and the solution with the smallest norm puts the mass close to the surface. The usual remedy is a depth weighting w = (depth + z0)^(-β/2) with β ≈ 2 (Li & Oldenburg, 1998), for which we solve for m = w .* ρ instead of ρ.

If you modify the forward function (e.g. a different parameterization), check that forward and adjoint are consistent with the dot-product test, as SimPEG does:

julia
ρ, r = randn(size(X)), randn(size(X, 1), size(X, 2))
sum(r .* forward(ρ)) ≈ sum(adjoint(r) .* ρ)     # must be true to ~1e-12

Example 3: least-squares inversion with Krylov.jl ​

The synthetic data are the anomaly of a block 300 kg/m³ denser than its surroundings, 10 km × 10 km wide, between 3 and 9 km depth, with 0.1 mGal of noise. Krylov.jl solves least-squares problems with iterative methods that only need products with the operator and its adjoint, which LinearMaps.jl provides:

julia
using LinearMaps, Krylov, LinearAlgebra

ρ_true = zeros(size(X))
ρ_true[(abs.(X) .< 5) .& (abs.(Y) .< 5) .& (-9 .< Z .< -3)] .= 300.0
dg_obs = forward(ρ_true) .+ 0.1 .* randn(size(X, 1), size(X, 2))

G = LinearMap(m -> vec(forward(reshape(m, size(X)))),           # density   -> anomaly
              r -> vec(adjoint(reshape(r, size(dg_obs)))),      # residual  -> density
              length(dg_obs), length(X))

w = vec((abs.(Z) .+ 1) .^ -1)                                    # depth weighting, z0 = 1 km, β = 2
m, stats = lsqr(G * Diagonal(1 ./ w), vec(dg_obs); itmax = 30)
ρ = reshape(m ./ w, size(X))

Without depth weighting (w = 1), the recovered density is concentrated just below the surface, as expected. With depth weighting, its maximum (133 kg/m³) lies at 6 km depth, the centre of the block. In both cases the rms misfit (0.065–0.075 mGal) is at the noise level, so both models explain the data equally well; which one is preferred is decided by the weighting, not by the data. Stop the iterations (itmax) or add damping (λ) once the misfit reaches the noise level, otherwise the noise is fitted too. As for every smooth gravity inversion, the recovered body is wider and has a smaller density contrast than the true one. 30 iterations take a few seconds.

The same can be done with Optim.jl, where the adjoint gives the gradient of the misfit. This is the way to go if you add your own regularization terms or a nonlinear parameterization (chain rule on top of the gradient below):

julia
using Optim
w = (abs.(Z) .+ 1) .^ -1
misfit(m) = sum(abs2, forward(m ./ w) - dg_obs) / 2                     # m = w .* ρ
gradient!(g, m) = (g .= adjoint(forward(m ./ w) - dg_obs) ./ w)
res = optimize(misfit, gradient!, zeros(size(X)), LBFGS(), Optim.Options(iterations = 30))
ρ = Optim.minimizer(res) ./ w

This gives the same result as LSQR. Bounds via Fminbox(LBFGS()) converged poorly in our tests (more than 100 s and still a 1.6 mGal misfit); for bounded problems use a projected method or a bounded parameterization as in Example 5.

Example 4: probabilistic inversion with Turing.jl ​

A probabilistic inversion samples the posterior distribution of the model parameters instead of computing a single model. This shows how well the parameters are constrained and how they trade off. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and its adaptive variant NUTS need the gradient of the log-posterior. If the forward problem is linear in the parameters and both prior and noise are Gaussian, the posterior is Gaussian and can be computed directly; HMC pays off for nonlinear parameterizations, bounds or non-Gaussian priors.

Since voxel_grav accepts densities of any number type, Turing.jl can differentiate it with its default automatic differentiation (ForwardDiff). This works well for models with a few parameters, e.g. the geometry and density of a body. Here we invert for the density contrast, top and thickness of the block of Example 3 (its lateral extent is assumed known). Its top and bottom faces are smoothed over 250 m, so that the anomaly is differentiable with respect to their depths:

julia
using GeophysicalModelGenerator, Turing, LinearAlgebra

X, Y, Z = xyz_grid(-20:2:20, -20:2:20, -12:0.5:0)                       # km
lateral = (abs.(X) .<= 5) .& (abs.(Y) .<= 5)
step(u) = 1 / (1 + exp(-u / 0.25))
block(Δρ, ztop, thick) = Δρ .* lateral .* step.(ztop .- Z) .* step.(Z .- (ztop - thick))
forward(ρ) = voxel_grav(X, Y, Z, ρ; refMod = zeros(size(X, 3)), lengthUnit = "km", rhoTol = 0, outName = nothing, printing = false)[1]

σ = 0.1                                                                  # data noise [mGal]
dg_obs = forward(block(300.0, -3.0, 6.0)) .+ σ .* randn(size(X, 1), size(X, 2))

@model function gravity_block(dg_obs)
    Δρ ~ Uniform(0, 1000)          # density contrast [kg/m³]
    ztop ~ Uniform(-10, -0.5)      # top of the block [km]
    thick ~ Uniform(0.5, 10)       # thickness [km]
    dg = forward(block(Δρ, ztop, thick))
    dg_obs ~ MvNormal(vec(dg), σ^2 * I)
end

chain = sample(gravity_block(vec(dg_obs)), NUTS(), 400)

The 400 samples took 2 minutes. The posterior means are close to the true values: density contrast 294 ± 9 kg/m³ (true 300), top -2.96 ± 0.07 km (true -3) and thickness 6.2 ± 0.2 km (true 6). The figure shows the classic non-uniqueness of gravity data: density contrast and thickness are almost perfectly anti-correlated (correlation -0.997), because the data constrain their product, i.e. the mass of the block (1805 ± 6 compared to 1800 kg/m³ × km). A thicker block must also have a higher top to keep its centre of mass at the same depth.

Example 5: one parameter per grid point with AdvancedHMC.jl ​

With thousands of parameters, e.g. a density for every grid point, forward-mode automatic differentiation is too slow, and the adjoint gives the gradient for the cost of one extra forward computation. AdvancedHMC.jl, the sampler used by Turing, accepts any log-density that implements the LogDensityProblems.jl interface. Here the density of every grid point is bounded between 0 and ρmax through a logistic function, with a Gaussian prior on the unbounded variable m:

julia
using GeophysicalModelGenerator, AdvancedHMC, AbstractMCMC, LogDensityProblems, Statistics

X, Y, Z = xyz_grid(-20:2:20, -20:2:20, -12:1:0)                        # km, 5733 grid points
forward(ρ) = voxel_grav(X, Y, Z, ρ; refMod = zeros(size(X, 3)), lengthUnit = "km", rhoTol = 0, outName = nothing, printing = false)[1]
adjoint(r) = voxel_grav_adjoint(X, Y, Z, r; lengthUnit = "km")
# dg_obs: observed anomaly, σ: its noise [mGal]

struct GravityPosterior end
ρmax = 500.0
logistic(m) = 1 / (1 + exp(-m))
LogDensityProblems.dimension(::GravityPosterior) = length(X)
LogDensityProblems.capabilities(::Type{GravityPosterior}) = LogDensityProblems.LogDensityOrder{1}()
function LogDensityProblems.logdensity_and_gradient(::GravityPosterior, m)
    s = logistic.(reshape(m, size(X)))
    r = forward(ρmax .* s) .- dg_obs
    logp = -sum(abs2, r) / (2σ^2) - sum(abs2, m) / (2 * 1.5^2)
    grad = -vec(adjoint(r) .* ρmax .* s .* (1 .- s)) ./ σ^2 .- m ./ 1.5^2    # chain rule: dρ/dm = ρmax s (1 - s)
    return logp, grad
end
LogDensityProblems.logdensity(p::GravityPosterior, m) = first(LogDensityProblems.logdensity_and_gradient(p, m))

samples = AbstractMCMC.sample(AbstractMCMC.LogDensityModel(GravityPosterior()), AdvancedHMC.NUTS(0.8), 200; n_adapts = 100, initial_params = fill(-3.0, length(X)))
ρs = [ρmax .* logistic.(reshape(s.z.θ, size(X))) for s in samples[101:end]]
ρ_mean, ρ_std = mean(ρs), std(ρs)

This runs (200 samples took 3 minutes for 5733 parameters, with 127 gradient evaluations per sample), but it is a recipe rather than a finished inversion. For the block of Example 3, these 200 samples are not converged (the misfit of the posterior mean is 0.3 mGal, three times the noise). Moreover, with the same prior for every grid point, the posterior puts the mass near the surface, like the least-squares solution without depth weighting. The Bayesian counterpart of depth weighting is a prior whose spread increases with depth; spatial correlation in the prior gives smoother models. Choose the prior for your problem and run much longer chains, checking convergence (e.g. with several chains and MCMCChains.jl).

Benchmark ​

We compared voxel_grav with five other codes on the same cell densities: a smooth 3D model with strong variations near the surface, either as rectangular prisms (flat Earth) or as tesseroids on a sphere of radius 6371 km. The stations lie above every grid column, 0.5 km (prisms) or 1 km (tesseroids) above the model.

  • Harmonica 0.7 (Python, numba): exact prism formulas (Nagy et al., 2000) and adaptive Gauss–Legendre quadrature for tesseroids (Uieda et al., 2016). We use it as the reference.

  • Tesseroids (C, compiled with -O3 as in its build script; the command-line tools prismgz and tessgz, whose times include reading and writing text files).

  • gravmagsubs 1.0.1 (R package around the Fortran prism routines of Plouff, USGS).

  • GMT 6.8 gravprisms (C, OpenMP), via GMT_jll.

  • SimPEG 0.26 with its default engine (Choclo, the prism kernels of Harmonica, numba), and with the geoana engine, which builds a dense sensitivity matrix.

Every code was run on an Apple M4 Max (12 performance and 4 efficiency cores, 40-core GPU), one after the other, with a warm-up run first (so that the compilation of Julia and numba is excluded), and the minimum of 3 runs is reported (1 run if it takes more than 30 s). The machine was shared with other jobs: the load average was 3–7 for most runs, but up to 90 for the 16-thread Harmonica runs of the large prism model, 60 for the small single-thread SimPEG run, and 8–16 for the 16-thread GMG runs of the large models; these may be somewhat slow. For voxel_grav we report the time for a new geometry (including the subdivision operator) and for repeated calls with the same geometry, as in inversions (operator stored).

Accuracy (maximum deviation from Harmonica, relative to the maximum anomaly, after converting the gravitational constants: Tesseroids 6.673e-11, gravmagsubs 6.674e-11, Harmonica, SimPEG and GMT 6.6743e-11, GMG 6.67408e-11 m³/(kg s²); SimPEG returns the upward component):

On one core, voxel_grav is 11 times faster than SimPEG and 36–80 times faster than the other prism codes for the large model, and 34–38 times faster than the tesseroid codes; the point mass with quadrupole is much cheaper than the exact prism formula or the quadrature of a tesseroid. Tests ​

Besides comparisons with the analytical solution for spheres, the tests (test/test_voxel_gravity.jl) include checks taken from other gravity codes: a large prism compared to the exact prism solution (Nagy et al., 2000) and convergence towards the infinite Bouguer slab (as in Harmonica), mirror symmetry and sign of the anomaly (as in Choclo), and the consistency of forward and adjoint operators, including a finite-difference check of the misfit gradient (as in SimPEG). They also check stations at different heights and arbitrary stations, the analytic gradients against finite differences, the cutoff, the adaptive subdivision, normal gravity (GRS80) and the Bouguer corrections, grids with variable spacing, that ForwardDiff through voxel_grav gives the same gradient as the adjoint, and that the KernelAbstractions kernels give the same results as the serial code. The ellipsoidal version is compared with the exact anomaly of a spherical cap and, for a small area, with the flat-Earth version. Curvilinear grids and topographic_effect are compared with an independent reference that integrates the cells as vertical line masses, and the second moments of inclined cells with a numerical integration.

GeophysicalModelGenerator.voxel_grav Function
julia
voxel_grav(X::Array{Float64, 3}, Y::Array{Float64, 3}, Z::Array{Float64, 3}, RHO::Array{<:Real, 3};
refMod="AVG", lengthUnit="m", rhoTol=1e-9, Topo=[], outName="Bouguer", printing=true, zRef=0,
backend=nothing, float_type=Float64, gradients=true, cutoff=Inf, subdivide=2.5)

Computes Bouguer anomalies and gradients

Required arguments:

  • X,Y,Z: 3D matrices with the coordinates of the grid (X should vary in the first dimension, Y in the second, Z (vertical) in the third). The spacing may vary along each direction.

  • RHO: 3D matrix with the density at each grid point [kg/m^3]. Any number type works, so that voxel_grav can be differentiated with ForwardDiff (with outName = nothing).

Optional arguments:

  • refMod: 1D vector with the reference density for each depth. Alternatively, the strings "NE", "SE", "SW", "NW", "AVG" can be used. In that case, one of the corners of RHO is used as reference model.In case of "AVG" the reference model is the average of each depth slice.

  • lengthUnit: The unit of the coordinates and topography file. Either "m" or "km"

  • rhoTol: density differences smaller than rhoTol will be ignored [kg/m^3]

  • Topo: 2D matrix with the topography of the surface, of size size(X)[1:2] (only relevant for the paraview output)

  • outName: name of the paraview output (do not include file type); nothing writes no file (e.g. inside an inversion loop)

  • printing: activate printing of additional information [true or false]

  • zRef: height of the stations at which the anomaly is computed (same unit as Z): a number, or an array of size size(X)[1:2] with the height of every surface point (e.g. the topography, see the free-air and Bouguer examples in the documentation). Only the part of the model below maximum(zRef) is taken into account.

  • backend: nothing (default) computes on one CPU core. A KernelAbstractions.jl backend computes in parallel, e.g. CPU() (all threads), MetalBackend() (Apple GPU, needs float_type = Float32) or CUDABackend(). Requires using KernelAbstractions (or a GPU package that loads it).

  • float_type: number type of the computation with a backend; Float32 is faster on GPUs but less accurate (relative error ≈ 1e-5).

  • gradients: compute the horizontal gradients of the anomaly; false saves time and returns nothing for them.

  • cutoff: ignore cells whose horizontal distance from a station is larger than cutoff (same unit as the coordinates); e.g. 166.7 km is the standard outer radius of terrain corrections. Default: all cells.

  • subdivide: cells closer to a station than subdivide times their size are split recursively into smaller point masses, which keeps the result accurate close to the stations; 0 switches this off.

Returns the anomaly dg [mGal] and its horizontal gradients gradX = ∂dg/∂x, gradY = ∂dg/∂y [mGal/m] at the surface grid points (x, y of the grid, z = zRef), computed analytically from the point masses. dg is linear in the density; voxel_grav_adjoint gives its adjoint (sensitivity).

source
julia
dg, dgdx, dgdy = voxel_grav(Grid::GeoData, RHO; refMod = "AVG", zRef = nothing, stations = nothing, rhoTol = 1e-9, gradients = false, cutoff = Inf,
                           subdivide = 2.5, ellipsoid = (a = 6378137.0, f = 1 / 298.257223563), backend = nothing, float_type = Float64)

Gravity anomaly [mGal] of the density RHO [kg/m³], given at the points of the regular geographic grid Grid (as created by lonlatdepth_grid), or of a curvilinear grid whose depth levels follow surfaces such as the topography (a 3D Depth array with monotonic columns, as built in topographic_effect).

The computation is done on the reference ellipsoid (WGS84 by default; ellipsoid = (a = 6371e3, f = 0) is a sphere). Depths and heights are heights above the ellipsoid in km; heights relative to sea level are used as such, i.e. the geoid undulation is ignored. Every cell is a point mass at its position on the ellipsoid (subdivided close to the stations), and dg is the component of its attraction along the ellipsoid normal at the station (downwards positive), i.e. the gravity disturbance caused by RHO - refMod.

  • refMod: reference density for every depth level, or "NE", "SE", "SW", "NW", "AVG" as in voxel_grav.

  • zRef: height of the stations above every grid column [km]: a number, or an array of size size(Grid.lon)[1:2], e.g. the topography. By default 0 (sea level), or the top of the grid for a curvilinear grid.

  • stations: GeoData with arbitrary station positions (e.g. measurement points; depth is their height in km), instead of the grid columns. zRef is then ignored.

  • gradients: also compute the horizontal gradients of dg towards east (dgdx) and north (dgdy) [mGal/m]; otherwise they are nothing.

  • cutoff: ignore cells whose horizontal distance from a station is larger than cutoff [km]; e.g. 166.7 km is the standard outer radius of terrain corrections. Default: all cells.

  • subdivide: cells closer to a station than subdivide times their size are split recursively into smaller point masses (0 switches this off).

  • rhoTol, backend, float_type: as in voxel_grav.

On a regular grid, only the part of the model below the highest station is used. The cells of a curvilinear grid are hexahedra with vertical edges, whose inclined top and bottom are taken into account by the full tensor of their second moments. dg, dgdx and dgdy have size size(Grid.lon)[1:2], or the size of stations.lon. voxel_grav_adjoint gives the adjoint of dg.

GeophysicalModelGenerator.voxel_grav_adjoint Function
julia
sens = voxel_grav_adjoint(X, Y, Z, R; lengthUnit = "m", zRef = 0, backend = nothing, float_type = Float64, cutoff = Inf, subdivide = 2.5)

Adjoint of voxel_grav. Returns sens, of the same size as X, with

julia
sens[i, j, k] = ∂(sum(R .* dg)) / ∂RHO[i, j, k]      [mGal per kg/m³]

where dg is the anomaly computed by voxel_grav(X, Y, Z, RHO; refMod, zRef) for a fixed reference-model vector refMod and R is a weight for every surface point (same size as dg). Since dg is linear in RHO, sens does not depend on RHO.

  • R = ones(size(dg)): sensitivity of the summed anomaly to the density of every grid point.

  • R = 1 at one station and 0 elsewhere: change of the anomaly at that station, δg ≈ sum(sens .* δRHO).

  • R = dg - dg_obs: gradient of the misfit sum((dg - dg_obs) .^ 2) / 2 with respect to RHO, for gradient-based inversion.

Grid points above maximum(zRef) have zero sensitivity. The cost is that of one call to voxel_grav with rhoTol = 0; backend, float_type, cutoff and subdivide work as there.

GeophysicalModelGenerator.topographic_effect Function
julia
te = topographic_effect(Topo; density = 2670, density_water = 1040, cutoff = 166.7, stations = nothing, kwargs...)

Complete Bouguer correction [mGal]: the attraction of the topography between sea level and the surface Topo with the density density (and of the oceans, as density_water - density between the sea floor and sea level), computed with voxel_grav at stations on the topography (or at sea level for the oceans), or at stations. Topo is a GeoData surface (computed on the ellipsoid) or a CartData surface (flat Earth), with the topography in km as depth/z, e.g. from import_topo.
